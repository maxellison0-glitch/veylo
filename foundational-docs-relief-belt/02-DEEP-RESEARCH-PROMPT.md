# DEEP RESEARCH PROMPT — Body-comfort and recovery device

*Reusable product-swappable prompt. Replace the product block and claim constraints; keep the question order and source rules.*

## Product block

- Product: The Veylo Relief Belt
- Market: United Kingdom
- Price: £69.99
- Format: flexible mains-powered wearable pad with 120 LEDs, specified at 660nm red and 850nm near-infrared
- Session: 20 minutes with automatic shut-off
- Intended areas: lower back, knee, shoulder and hip
- Brand position: “technology, without the theatre”
- Claim constraint: comfort, routine and wellbeing language only; no medical claims, pain-relief promises, “clinical”, treatment or cure language
- Proof constraint: no invented reviews, no before-and-after images, no borrowed outcomes from devices or protocols that are not this product
- Research date: 30 August 2026

## Assignment

Research the real UK buyer for this product. Do not begin from a brand persona. Begin from current first-person language and work outward.

Produce a source-linked research document of at least six pages. Separate:

1. what buyers say;
2. what current evidence establishes;
3. what competitors claim;
4. what is a strategic inference.

Never present a competitor claim as scientific evidence. Never transfer evidence from a laser, panel, professional device or different dose to the product above. When evidence conflicts, put the conflict in the document.

## Source priority

Use sources in this order:

1. Direct Mumsnet threads, especially Chronic Pain, General Health, Exercise and Chat.
2. Direct Reddit threads in red-light, recovery, sport and condition-specific communities.
3. Verified buyer reviews on named competitor pages and Trustpilot, including low-star reviews.
4. Current official UK product pages for price and specification.
5. NHS, HSE, Sport England and other UK public bodies for context.
6. Peer-reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses, preferably PubMed records and full papers.
7. Museum, scientific-institution or primary historical sources for pre-1960 history.

Search summaries are discovery aids, not quote sources where the underlying page can be opened.

## Source rules

- Every quotation must be verbatim and attributed to a real username, named reviewer or named publication.
- Record title, platform, URL and date for every quoted source.
- Keep quotes short enough to preserve context without reproducing whole posts.
- Mark commercial and affiliate sources.
- Record current prices in pounds and the access date.
- Do not invent demographic precision unsupported by the source set.
- Do not call a self-selected forum sample representative of the UK population.
- Treat self-reported outcomes as experience, not evidence.
- Identify sponsored, promotional or seller-authored posts where visible.

## Questions — answer in this order

### 1. Demographic and psychographic

- Who is researching body-comfort and recovery devices in the UK?
- What ages, genders, occupations and activity patterns appear repeatedly?
- Which two segments are meaningfully different: recurring everyday discomfort versus post-training recovery?
- Which segment is more likely to buy a £69.99 wearable light device from cold Meta traffic?
- What do they believe about ageing, strength, work, movement, rest and self-reliance?
- What are their economic attitudes: cheap trial, premium proof, one-off device or recurring professional spend?
- What are their hopes and dreams beneath “feel better”?
- Which lost activities do they name?
- What counts as a victory?
- What do they blame: work posture, age, injury, waiting lists, inconsistent routines, misleading marketing or themselves?
- What prejudices do they hold about cheap devices and paid reviews?
- Distil their beliefs about life and recovery into one to three sentences.

### 2. Existing solutions

List what they already use with current UK prices:

- heat packs and hot-water bottles;
- TENS devices and replacement pads;
- massage guns;
- stretching, yoga, Pilates and strength work;
- sports massage and private physiotherapy;
- red-light panels, wraps and targeted devices;
- doing nothing and waiting.

For every category answer:

- What do users like?
- What do they dislike?
- What does it require them to set up, hold, charge, book or replace?
- How quickly do they expect to notice anything?
- What makes them stop?
- What creates buyer's remorse?
- What breaks?
- What hidden recurring costs appear?
- What are the strongest horror stories?
- Does the market think the solution works?
- If not, exactly why not?

Pay particular attention to abandonment after purchase. Search explicitly for: “under the bed”, “gathering dust”, “stopped working”, “battery”, “cables”, “pads”, “too hot”, “hard to reach”, “subtle”, “not sure”, “worth it” and “regret”.

### 3. Product-category evidence

Find the strongest recent systematic reviews for:

- photobiomodulation and non-specific low-back pain;
- photobiomodulation and exercise recovery or muscle soreness;
- dose response and why wavelength alone is not enough.

Report study count, participant count, certainty and conclusion where the paper provides them. Explain the implication for consumer copy. Do not cherry-pick a positive result around a stronger negative review.

### 4. Curiosity and pre-1960 attempts

- Find a verifiable pre-1960 history of red or infrared light that competitors are not all using.
- Prefer a scientific discovery story over a cure story.
- Assess William Herschel's 1800 prism-and-thermometer discovery.
- Explain what it proves — invisible infrared exists — and what it does not prove — that a consumer belt changes a medical outcome.
- Check whether the history offers a useful metaphor for invisible technology without becoming theatre.

### 5. Corruption or outside forces

Ask honestly:

- Is the discomfort new, or are the work patterns and category economics new?
- What do HSE data say about work-related musculoskeletal complaints?
- How do affiliate reviews, unreported device specs and “clinical-grade” language make the market harder to trust?
- Who profits from premium batteries, apps, replacement pads, recurring appointments and product churn?
- Is there any real suppression story? If not, say no.

### 6. Language bank

Collect:

- at least 30 short verbatim problem phrases;
- at least 20 short failed-solution phrases;
- at least 10 real thread titles that function as pre-tested headlines;
- phrases about the desired state: loose, mobile, ready, normal, back to walking, able to sit, able to train, quiet, comfortable and rested.

Keep medical and highly acute language in the research bank but flag it as unsuitable for Veylo copy.

### 7. Objections

Rank by observed frequency:

- “Does it do anything?”
- “Where are the output and dose specs?”
- “Is this the same cheap belt sold elsewhere?”
- “Why is it cheaper than premium devices?”
- “Why is it not cordless?”
- “Will the cables annoy me?”
- “Will it fit?”
- “Will I use it for twenty minutes?”
- “Is it safe for me?”
- “Does it replace physio or medical care?”
- “What if it is not right for me?”

Give an honest answer to each using only product facts, public policy and the research.

## Final synthesis

End with:

1. one paragraph titled “What this research says”;
2. the single most defensible buyer;
3. the unique mechanism of the problem;
4. the named unique mechanism of the product format;
5. the North Star belief;
6. a claims table with columns: sourced, inference, unsupported and prohibited;
7. the highest-value next action.

Do not write sales copy inside the research document. Build the argument the copy will later follow.
