# NECESSARY BELIEFS — The Relief Belt

*Maximum six. Each belief must be held before the offer appears. They are not product benefits and do not require a medical claim.*

---

## 1

> **I believe that the end-of-day routine I repeat matters more than the recovery product I intend to use.**

This is the entry belief. The market is already full of plausible options. Failure usually happens between intention and setup: finding the pads, charging the battery, holding the tool, doing the exercises or booking the next visit.

Evidence to build:

- TENS placement and replacement friction;
- massage-gun reach;
- cable and battery complaints;
- devices found under the bed;
- “use the method that you would use the most”.

Do not turn this into blame. The insight is that format shapes consistency.

---

## 2

> **I believe that a comfort ritual can be worthwhile without pretending to be medical treatment.**

This is the category reset.

The buyer has been trained to expect “clinical-grade”, “deep healing” and fast relief. The research does not support those claims for this product. She must accept a narrower, more credible role:

- a deliberate pause;
- a targeted light routine;
- a hands-free, timed session;
- a small part of wellbeing, alongside movement and appropriate professional care.

This belief protects conversion from post-purchase disappointment. The product is judged against the offer it actually made.

---

## 3

> **I believe that the simplest device can be the most useful device when it is always ready.**

Mains power looks like a missing feature if the page leaves it unexplained.

The buyer must understand the trade:

- she gives up cordless movement;
- she removes charging, battery ageing, app setup and one major failure point.

The proof is not that batteries are always bad. It is that premium device owners repeatedly report battery inconvenience and failure, while a sofa-side use case does not require portability.

---

## 4

> **I believe that wavelength is a specification, not a guarantee.**

This is the trust belief.

660nm and 850nm tell the buyer what light is specified. They do not establish output, dose, depth or a body outcome. The page earns trust by refusing to turn two numbers into “clinical-grade”.

What has to be shown:

- exact known specifications;
- the missing measurements acknowledged internally and tested next;
- no borrowed study outcome;
- no “same as the clinic” language;
- a plain statement that the belt is not medical treatment.

This belief keeps Veylo from selling education that sends the buyer to the most aggressive competitor. Candour becomes part of the mechanism.

---

## 5

> **I believe that a £69.99 light wrap can cost less because it does less, not because the seller is hiding something.**

The price is currently suspicious because the category's premium wearable devices range from £209 to £495.

The buyer needs the price gap explained:

- no rechargeable battery;
- no app;
- no vibration system;
- no dedicated heating function;
- no laser modules;
- one flexible LED pad, strap and timer.

Do not claim equal performance. The belief is that a narrower device can be an intentional purchase.

---

## 6

> **I believe that I can judge this company by the limits it publishes as well as the claims it makes.**

Veylo has no genuine Relief Belt review base and no product-specific trial. The page cannot argue the buyer into social proof.

It can show:

- no invented reviews;
- no before-and-after theatre;
- who should seek advice before use;
- the socket-side limitation;
- adjustable one-size wording instead of “fits all”;
- 30-day return terms;
- visible UK contact and dispatch information;
- independent optical testing when completed.

This belief is earned by the page's behaviour, not stated as a slogan.

---

# The North Star

> ## I do not need another recovery promise to chase. I need a simple comfort ritual I will actually repeat.

Every page line should move the buyer toward that conclusion:

- the lead recognises the accumulated day;
- the mechanism removes Recovery Admin;
- the product becomes The Sofa Session;
- the plug becomes a deliberate trade-off;
- the price is anchored inside the category;
- the evidence boundary is explicit.

If a line requires the buyer to believe the belt relieves pain, treats a joint, speeds healing or reproduces a clinic, it moves away from the North Star and must be cut.

---

# What each belief requires

| Belief | What must be built now |
|---|---|
| 1 — repeat beats intend | Lead on the evening routine; show sofa-side, hands-free use |
| 2 — comfort without treatment | Replace pain language with comfort and wellbeing; add an honest-limit FAQ |
| 3 — ready beats feature-heavy | Explain mains power as no charging and no battery, while naming the socket constraint |
| 4 — wavelength is not outcome | Remove “clinical-grade” and tissue-depth claims; add visible red / less-visible NIR explanation |
| 5 — lower price has a reason | Anchor £69.99 against £209–£495 premium wearables and explain omitted systems |
| 6 — limits create trust | Remove “Bestseller” and “fits all”; keep contraindications and return terms clear |

---

# What has to be built next

| Proof gap | Next asset |
|---|---|
| Irradiance and dose | Independent optical test at intended placement |
| Wavelength accuracy | Measured tolerance report |
| Fit | Strap circumference range |
| Sourcing trust | Plain-English sourcing, QC and UK support note |
| Real social proof | Post-purchase request to verified buyers |
| Consistency | A real 20 Sofa Sessions card or email sequence |

---

# What must never be built

Anything that needs a seventh belief:

> “I believe this untested consumer belt clinically relieves my pain.”

That belief is unsupported, prohibited by the brand and unnecessary to sell the product's honest role.
