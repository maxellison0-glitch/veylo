**Medium to high.**

Until those exist, do not add performance adjectives to the mechanism.

## Guru

### William Herschel (1738–1822)

Herschel is a better historical guide than the category's exhausted NASA story.

In 1800 he split sunlight through a prism and measured the temperature of its colours. The thermometer warmed progressively toward red. He then moved it beyond the visible red edge, where no light could be seen, and recorded an even higher temperature. He had detected infrared radiation.

Why he fits:

- He was an astronomer measuring, not a salesman promising.
- The story explains invisible light with a physical instrument.
- It supports the brand voice: technology, without the theatre.
- It does not require a medical claim.

What he does not do:

- endorse the product;
- prove an effect in tissue;
- establish that 850nm LEDs deliver a useful dose.

The guru's role is to make the invisible understandable, not to borrow authority.

Sources: [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co3632/william-herschels-infrared-prism), [NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/07_infraredwaves/), [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Herschel/Caroline_and_William_Herschel_Revealing_the_invisible).

## Discovery Story

> In 1800, William Herschel put a thermometer somewhere the light appeared to end. It warmed anyway. The useful part of the experiment was not that the radiation was mysterious. It was that he measured what he could not see.
>
> Two centuries later, wearable-light marketing often does the reverse: it shows glowing LEDs and asks the customer to imagine the measurement. Veylo should not.
>
> The Relief Belt starts with the facts it has: 120 LEDs, 660nm red, 850nm near-infrared, a flexible pad and a 20-minute timer. It leaves out the medical promise it has not earned. The point is a simple, repeatable light ritual — not a clinic on the sofa.

This story belongs lower on a future long-form page or technology section. It is not the cold-traffic lead.

## Objections with Honest Answers

### 1. “Does it actually work?”

It provides the listed red and near-infrared light in a close-fitting 20-minute format. Veylo does not guarantee a medical outcome. Red-light evidence varies by device, dose, condition and protocol; this product has not been clinically tested.

### 2. “Where is the irradiance?”

Not currently published. This is the offer's largest proof gap. Commission independent measurement and publish it.

### 3. “Is it just a cheap marketplace belt?”

The page must say what Veylo specifies, inspects and supports. Do not imply exclusive hardware if there is none. Brand trust comes from UK support, clear instructions, delivery, return terms and technical disclosure.

### 4. “Why is it cheaper than Therabody, Kineon or Lumaflex?”

It is a simpler product. It does not include a rechargeable battery, app, vibration motor, heating system or laser modules. The lower price reflects that narrower build.

### 5. “Why is it plugged in?”

The cable limits use to a nearby socket. In return, there is no battery to charge, age or replace. State both sides.

### 6. “Twenty minutes is too long.”

Do not argue. It is a twenty-minute session. The format is hands-free and timed, so it can sit inside television, reading or an evening pause.

### 7. “Can I walk around in it?”

It is mains-powered and should be treated as a socket-side product. The stronger use case is sitting or reclining safely near the outlet, following the supplied guide.

### 8. “Will it fit?”

It has an adjustable one-size strap. Publish the supported circumference once measured. Until then, customer support should answer before purchase.

### 9. “Can I wear it over clothing?”

Follow the supplied product instructions. The page should recommend clean, dry skin unless final safety documentation allows another use.

### 10. “Is it suitable for me?”

No device is suitable for everyone. Avoid broken or irritated skin. Ask a qualified professional if pregnant, using photosensitising medication, living with an implanted electronic device or uncertain.

### 11. “Does it replace physio, medication or assessment?”

No. It is a wellbeing and comfort ritual.

### 12. “What if I do not like it?”

The 30-day return terms apply. Read the hygiene-seal conditions before opening.

### 13. “Why should I trust a brand with no reviews?”

Do not invent the missing proof. Publish the specs, limits, company contact, dispatch process and return route. Ask genuine purchasers for honest reviews after use.

## Other Notes

### What the research contradicts

The original positioning treats 660/850nm as sufficient evidence for pain and deeper-muscle claims. It is not. The strongest low-back systematic review found no clinically important effect, and the product lacks output data. The rewrite must remove those claims.

### Why the simpler product can still win

It does not need to outperform £479 hardware. It needs to be:

- clear about its narrower role;
- easier to understand;
- visibly easier to keep ready;
- priced in context;
- supported by real UK fulfilment and a usable return route.

### Single next proof investment

Independent optical testing. Until irradiance and dose are known, every additional efficacy sentence is guesswork. After testing, Veylo can decide whether the hardware supports a stronger technical proposition or whether the product should remain a pure comfort ritual.
