# SALES PAGE TEARDOWN — The Relief Belt

*Reviewed 30 August 2026 against the live product object in lib/catalog.ts and the shared product-page template. The live Meta ad is doing its job; this review treats the page as the conversion problem.*

## What is already good

The page has a useful factual core. It gives a real price, exact wavelengths, an LED count, product dimensions, weight, plug type, intended session length and an automatic timer. The buyer can tell what arrives and can picture using it on a lower back, knee, shoulder or hip. Free UK delivery and the 30-day guarantee are visible beside the purchase control. Those are better foundations than most unbranded marketplace listings.

The physical format is also genuinely differentiated:

- A flexible wrap keeps the light close and steady without asking the user to hold a device.
- Mains power removes charging, battery ageing and app setup.
- The 20-minute auto-off timer makes the end of the session automatic.
- At 500g, the pad is materially lighter than premium multi-therapy back wraps such as Therabody's 1.8kg ThermBack LED.

The page does not need a new product. It needs to turn those facts into an honest argument.

## The immediate compliance problem

The current entry crosses Veylo's own line repeatedly:

- “wrapped around wherever it hurts”
- “therapy belt”
- “leaves muscles feeling looser and joints less stiff”
- “clinical-grade wavelengths”
- “targeting both surface tissue and deeper muscle”
- “the area you want to treat”
- concern: “Pain relief”

“Clinical-grade” is undefined and must be removed. The remaining phrases imply a treatment outcome the product page cannot substantiate. A 2020 systematic review of 12 randomised trials, pooled n=1,046, found no clinically important pain or disability benefit for photobiomodulation in non-specific low-back pain. More recent recovery evidence is encouraging in places but low-certainty and based on studied protocols that cannot simply be transferred to this consumer belt.

The correct page can explain what the hardware does. It cannot promise what a body will do next.

The “Bestseller” badge is also unsupported by the handoff data: the paid campaign has produced zero sales. It should not remain.

## There is no lead

The page begins with:

> 120 LEDs of red and near-infrared light, wrapped around wherever it hurts.

That is a specification plus a medical implication. It does not begin with the person arriving from the ad:

- the worker whose lower back marks the end of a long sitting day;
- the active adult who wants a quiet post-training wind-down;
- the person tired of holding a massage gun at an awkward angle;
- the buyer who wants a routine that can happen on the sofa without another appointment.

Cold traffic has not come to compare nanometres. It has come because the image recognised a moment in its day. The page should continue that moment before it explains the device.

## There is no defensible promise

The current page offers an implied outcome — looser muscles, less-stiff joints, deeper targeting — but no disciplined promise.

The strongest claim available is behavioural, not medical:

> Twenty quiet minutes for the places that carry the day.

That promise is true before a sale is made. The buyer gets:

- a targeted, close-fitting red and near-infrared light routine;
- a hands-free session;
- no app, battery or charging schedule;
- a fixed end point;
- a single £69.99 purchase backed by 30 days to change their mind.

This is not weaker than a pain claim. In a category crowded with “fast-acting relief”, “deep healing” and “clinical-grade” language, a precise comfort promise is the credible alternative.

## It is a catalogue entry, not a sales argument

The page currently moves from strapline to price to a paragraph of features, then into the standard purchase controls and accordions. The sequence is:

> object → specifications → instructions

The sequence cold traffic needs is:

> recognisable day → smaller, repeatable ritual → why this format → honest limits → price in context → purchase

Because the shared template must not be changed without Max's approval, the Relief Belt entry itself has to carry that sequence:

1. Strapline: identify the end-of-day or post-training moment.
2. Description: make the routine visible and remove the medical claim.
3. Long description: name the format and explain why it is easier to repeat.
4. Feature list and technology section: turn specifications into proof of the routine.
5. FAQs: handle evidence, power-cable, fit, suitability and returns objections.
6. Price anchor: compare £69.99 with the premium wearable-light category, not with healthcare.

## Proof: what exists and what does not

### Proof the page can use

- 120 LEDs.
- 660nm red and 850nm near-infrared specifications.
- Flexible pad.
- 20-minute automatic timer.
- Mains power.
- 500g weight.
- UK plug.
- 30-day return terms.
- Current market prices: Therabody ThermBack LED £209; Kineon MOVE+ £479; Lumaflex Body Pro £495 on 30 August 2026.
- The product can be shown in use on the body areas named in its instructions.

### Proof the page does not have

- Product-specific clinical testing.
- Verified irradiance or dose at skin.
- Genuine Veylo customer reviews.
- Evidence that this belt relieves pain, reduces stiffness, reaches a specific tissue depth or speeds healing.
- Evidence for “one size fits all”.
- Evidence for the “Bestseller” label.

The page should not borrow an effect measured with a different laser, LED array, dose or treatment protocol. Wavelength alone is not equivalence.

## The mechanism is unnamed

The current copy lists red light, near-infrared light, a strap and a timer. That leaves the buyer comparing LED count and price.

The honest unique mechanism is the delivery format:

### The Sofa Session

One flexible, close-fitting pad. One strap. One button. One automatic 20-minute finish.

The mechanism does not claim that light cures anything. It explains why this device has a chance of becoming a routine when other purchases do not:

- A massage gun has to be held and aimed.
- TENS pads have to be placed and periodically replaced.
- Premium cordless wraps have to be charged and introduce battery failure.
- Large panels need space and positioning.
- Appointments require travel, scheduling and recurring spend.

The Relief Belt asks the user to fasten it and sit down. The session runs while the evening continues.

## Objections are unhandled

The real buyer questions are not answered:

1. “Does it actually do anything?”
2. “Is this another overhyped red-light product?”
3. “Why is it much cheaper than the £200–£500 devices?”
4. “Where is the irradiance or dose?”
5. “Will I keep using it?”
6. “Why is it plugged in?”
7. “Will the strap fit me?”
8. “Can I use it through clothing?”
9. “Is it suitable with pregnancy, medication or an implanted device?”
10. “What happens if it is not right for me?”

The honest answers convert better than deflection:

- No medical outcome is guaranteed.
- Veylo publishes only the product specifications it can verify.
- The lower price reflects a simpler device: light, flexible pad, mains power and timer; no app, rechargeable battery, vibration motor or heating system.
- Mains power is a trade-off: use it near a socket, but never wait for it to charge.
- Fit is adjustable, not universal.
- Follow the supplied instructions and use on clean, dry skin unless directed otherwise.
- The standard contraindication and return guidance applies.

## £69.99 is floating without context

The old page asks the buyer to compare £69.99 with doing nothing. That is always a difficult comparison.

The defensible comparison is the same product class:

| Current UK option, 30 Aug 2026 | Price |
|---|---:|
| Veylo Relief Belt | £69.99 |
| Therabody ThermBack LED | £209 sale / £249 list |
| Kineon MOVE+ LED & Laser | £479 |
| Lumaflex Body Pro | £495 |

The page should not imply equivalence: those premium products add batteries, lasers, heat, vibration, apps or verified output specifications. The point is narrower:

> Premium wearable-light devices currently begin above £200. The Relief Belt is £69.99 because it keeps the routine simple.

That anchors the price while telling the truth about the trade-off.

## The twenty minutes are being sold incorrectly

The current page says “Twenty minutes a day is all it takes.” That is a medical-result implication and treats a real friction as trivial.

The buyer does not need to be told twenty minutes is nothing. They need to be shown where it fits:

- one television episode;
- reading on the sofa;
- an end-of-day cup of tea;
- a quiet post-training pause.

The session is not short. It is hands-free and bounded. That is the better promise.

## Recommended rewrite direction

### Lead

> Twenty quiet minutes for the places that carry the day.

### Core argument

Work, training and long hours sitting leave people wanting a deliberate wind-down. Most recovery tools create another job: book it, hold it, aim it, charge it or replace its pads. The Relief Belt is the simpler routine — fasten one flexible 120-LED pad around the selected area, press start and let the timer finish.

### Named mechanism

> The Sofa Session

### Price anchor

> £69.99 once. Premium wearable-light devices currently start above £200.

### Honest limit

> Built for everyday comfort and wellbeing. It is not a medical treatment and does not replace professional advice.

## Verdict

The product has enough specific, ownable facts to sell without a medical promise. The conversion problem is that the page currently leads with the promise it cannot prove and buries the routine it can. Move the argument from “clinical light for pain” to “the simplest targeted light ritual in the category”, make the £69.99 trade-off explicit, and let restraint become the proof.
